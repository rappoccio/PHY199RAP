## The framing question
If wind and solar are the cheapest new electricity ever built (Lecture 2's LCOE slides) — and
that's **before** counting a single subsidy or externality — then why did fossil fuels win the
market for a century? The honest answer isn't "physics" or "the free market." It's that the
fossil "price" has never included the parts of the bill someone else pays: the health damage, the
climate damage, and a century of public money and political muscle keeping it cheap on paper.

**Thesis for the session:** "Cheaper" is a question about *who pays which parts of the bill.*
Fossil fuels look cheap on the invoice because their biggest costs are pushed onto the public
(implicit subsidies) and their favorable treatment is defended by spending most industries can't
match (lobbying). Put the whole bill on one ledger and the "cheap" story inverts.

This is also a **skill** payoff: the course keeps teaching *scrutinize the number, ask what it
leaves out.* LCOE left out subsidies and externalities on purpose; this session adds them back.

## The data spine (primary sources)

### 1. The subsidy number — IMF (international body; strongest anchor per source-integrity rule)
- **Global fossil-fuel subsidies hit a record ~$7 trillion in 2022** — about **7.1% of global GDP**.
  - **Explicit** subsidies (governments directly undercharging vs. supply cost): **~$1.3 trillion**.
  - **Implicit** subsidies (not charging for the damage — local air pollution, global warming,
    congestion — plus forgone consumption-tax revenue): **~$5.7 trillion**, the bulk of the total.
  - Explicit support **spiked 2020→2022** as governments capped consumer energy prices during the
    price shock.
  - Source: **IMF Working Paper WP/23/169**, *"IMF Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data: 2023 Update"*
    (Black, Liu, Parry, Vernon, Aug 2023); IMF blog *"Fossil Fuel Subsidies Surged to Record
    $7 Trillion."*
  - Independent corroboration: **Our World in Data**, *"How much in subsidies do fossil fuels
    receive?"* (walks through the explicit-vs-implicit method).

- **Teach the nuance (for defensibility in Q&A):** critics correctly note the ~$7T is mostly
  **implicit** — unpriced externalities, *not* cash handed to oil companies. That's a fair
  distinction, and it *sharpens* the argument rather than weakening it: even the **explicit,
  cash-and-tax-break** number is **~$1.3T/yr**, and the implicit $5.7T is exactly the "external
  cost" LCOE and the L1 death-cost slide already told us is real — it's the same bill, now
  itemized. Don't overclaim "$7T in checks to Exxon"; say "$1.3T in direct support **plus** ~$5.7T
  in damage the public eats." (A skeptic write-up making the "not a real subsidy" case: Art Berman
  — useful to pre-empt, not to endorse.)

### 2. The narrower, explicit-only tallies (for cross-checking)
- **IEA** and **OECD** track mostly **explicit** support (consumption + production subsidies); their
  headline numbers run in the **hundreds of billions per year**, lower than the IMF total because
  they exclude implicit/externality costs. *(Verify the current IEA/OECD figure at build — it moves
  year to year with energy prices.)* Use these to show the range depends entirely on what you count.

### 3. The lobbying machine — OpenSecrets (nonprofit; the "who defends it" half)
- **US oil & gas spent $124.4 million on federal lobbying in 2022** (a record-profit year), and
  **~$72 million in just the first half of 2024**.
  - Source: **OpenSecrets** — *Oil & Gas lobbying profile* (industry id E01) and their 2023/2024
    reporting.
- **Coordinated obstruction, not just spending:** **InfluenceMap** documents how major oil & gas
  companies and trade groups (e.g., the **American Petroleum Institute**) have lobbied to weaken
  climate policy for decades — the same talking points recycled since the 1990s (OpenSecrets'
  API analysis makes this point too).
- **The reframe:** industry lobbying (~$100M/yr) is *tiny* next to the subsidies and tax
  treatment it defends (explicit support in the hundreds of billions to >$1T). That asymmetry —
  spend millions to protect hundreds of billions — is the "companies can afford lobbyists" point,
  quantified. (Let students compute the ratio themselves; don't assert a specific ROI without a
  cited figure.)

### 4. Historical incumbency (why "the market chose it" is incomplete)
- US oil & gas has enjoyed **structural tax preferences since 1913** (the intangible-drilling-cost
  deduction; the percentage-depletion allowance) — over a century of a public thumb on the scale
  before renewables existed. Anchor with an academic/tax-policy history, not an advocacy page.

## Simple calculations (course-style)
- **$7T ÷ ~8 billion people ≈ $875 per person per year** the world spends subsidizing fossil fuels.
- **$7T ÷ ~$105T global GDP ≈ 6.7–7%** of everything the world produces.
- **Explicit $1.3T** vs. what the energy transition needs annually — put them side by side.
- **Lobbying leverage:** $124M US oil & gas lobbying vs. the US explicit fossil support it defends
  → let students find the ratio (millions defending billions). Flag it as *their* calc to source.
- Tie-back: recompute Lecture 2's coal LCOE with the **death cost** (L1: coal ≈ deadliest per TWh,
  Markandya & Wilkinson 2007) and a carbon price added — watch "cheap" coal move.

## Slide skeleton (Sal's voice: dated/chronological titles, blunt, one idea per slide)
1. **The setup (callback):** "Lecture 2 said wind & solar are cheapest — *without* subsidies. So why did we burn coal for 150 years?"
2. **1913: the thumb on the scale** — a century of oil & gas tax preferences before renewables existed.
3. **Two kinds of subsidy** — explicit (cash & tax breaks) vs. implicit (the damage nobody's billed for). Define clearly.
4. **$1.3 trillion** — the explicit, cash-on-the-barrel number (IMF).
5. **$7 trillion** — add the implicit damage and it's ~7% of world GDP (IMF). *This is the L1 death cost + carbon cost, itemized.*
6. **"That's not a real subsidy!"** — steel-man the critique, then show it still leaves $1.3T explicit + a very real damage bill. (Skill: scrutinize the number.)
7. **$124 million/year** — US oil & gas federal lobbying (OpenSecrets); the API's decades-old playbook (InfluenceMap).
8. **Millions to protect billions** — the lobbying-leverage asymmetry; who can afford to rig the comparison.
9. **Re-run the race honestly** — coal/gas LCOE + death cost + carbon + minus the subsidy → the "cheap" story inverts.
10. **Takeaway:** "Cheapest" is a question about *who pays the parts of the bill that don't show up on the bill." Forward to Wks 3–4 (political implications).

**Motif hook (optional, continues the deck's style):** the fossil "price tag" as a magician's
misdirection — the cheap number on the sticker, the real cost in the other hand.
